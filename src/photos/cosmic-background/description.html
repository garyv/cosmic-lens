Look around: you are standing inside the oldest photograph possible. The faint, mottled glow on every side of you is the afterimage of creation. The pattern of light and dark is not starfields or nebulae but tiny temperature differences—fractions of a degree—that were imprinted on the universe roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Those minute ripples are the primordial blueprint from which all later structure, from gas clouds to galaxies and clusters, grew.

The map was made in a Mollweide projection: the whole celestial sphere flattened into a wide oval so the global pattern can be seen at once. Here it is wrapped back around you, the way this light really reaches us from every direction. Drag to look anywhere, even straight up or down. It starts the way astronomers draw it, with the plane of our galaxy level and its center straight ahead.

Rather than bright points, the map is a grainy quilt of blotches and streaks; the contrasts are vanishingly small, typically measured in microkelvin (millionths of a degree) against a nearly uniform background temperature. The image’s color scales to encode the tiny hot and cold deviations that carry the universe’s earliest information.

The Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) was a dedicated spacecraft that measured microwave radiation arriving from every direction on the sky. Stationed near the Earth–Sun L2 point and operating across multiple microwave frequency bands for nine years, WMAP’s instruments recorded the faint thermal glow left over from the hot, opaque early universe. To pull the primordial signal out of foreground noise—emission from our own Galaxy, point sources, and local interference—scientists combined data from different frequencies and applied careful foreground subtraction and internal combination techniques. The result is a cleaned, full-sky map of the CMB’s anisotropy: the ancient, fossilized pattern of temperature known as the cosmic microwave background.

The scientific meaning is profound and precise. The characteristic sizes and amplitudes of the fluctuations in this map let cosmologists measure the universe’s age, composition and geometry: the WMAP nine-year analysis helped pin the age of the universe at about 13.77 billion years and revealed the acoustic peaks that support the theory of early cosmic inflation and a universe dominated by dark energy and dark matter. In short, this quiet, grainy map is a census of the seeds from which the cosmic web and all visible structure emerged.
